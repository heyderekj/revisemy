# Show, don't tell

The most common note on a homepage is "too much text, not enough visuals". Makers know their product so well that they describe it. Visitors don't read descriptions. They look for something that shows them.

## Hard rules

1. **Every section gets a visual that carries its point,** except the FAQ and pricing fine print. Cover the text. If you can still tell what the section is about, it passes.
2. **The product comes first.** The best visual for a software homepage is almost always the software. Use real UI, with real-looking content, before reaching for anything else.
3. **No stock or AI imagery standing in for the product,** and never AI images with readable text in them. They cost more trust than they add.
4. **Fewer words once there's a visual.** When you add a visual, cut the paragraph it replaces down to a heading and one line.
5. **Use the product's own vocabulary:** its icons, its colors, its type. The homepage should preview the app, not a generic one.

## The visual forms

Pick the form that fits what the section is trying to say.

| The section says… | Show it with |
| --- | --- |
| "Here's what the app does" | A real screenshot or a stylized slice of the UI, cropped to the part that matters. Not the whole window |
| "It works on your phone" | A device mockup, one or two screens, not a fan of six |
| "Here's what's included" | An icon grid: the app's own icons, a short heading, one line each. Three to six items |
| "Here's how it works" | Three or four steps, each with a small UI crop or icon |
| "Here's what you'll get" | A mockup of the outcome: the calendar with the plan filled in, the inbox with the first email, the finished document |
| "Here's who it's for" | Real photography of real people in the setting, or a parent-and-child, team-or-group visual that matches the audience |
| "Here's what's different" | A before and after, or a side-by-side comparison |
| "Here's the feeling" | Mixed media: traditional imagery (paintings, paper, handwriting, texture) next to crisp UI. Keep one system so it feels intentional |
| "Here's the brand" | The app icon large and centered, with assets around it, a short heading and a straight paragraph |

**Stylized UI** means a simplified, on-brand rendering of the interface: real layout, fewer details, bigger type. It reads at a glance where a full screenshot doesn't.

## Workflow

1. **Inventory.** List each section with its word count and what visual it has now (none, decorative, product).
2. **Flag** every section over about 40 words with no visual that carries the point, and every visual that's decorative only, such as a background photo or a stock image.
3. **Pick a form** for each flagged section from the table. Say what exactly it shows — which screen, which content, which crop.
4. **Cut the copy** that the visual makes unnecessary. Write the replacement heading and line.
5. **Build it** if asked: crop or recreate the UI, set it on a consistent grid, and keep the visual language the same across sections.

## Required output format

| Section | Now | Show instead | Copy after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <name> | <what's there> | <the form, and exactly what it shows> | <new heading / line> |

Then up to three lines on the system that ties the visuals together: shared frame, crop style, background and spacing.

## Tone

Practical. Say exactly which screen, which crop, which icon. "Add a visual" isn't feedback. "A cropped phone screen of the plan for day 12, with the reading and the check-in button" is.
