# Homepage craft

These are rules for the homepage of an app or tool. They come from real feedback on real landing pages, where the same notes kept coming back: too much text, the product hidden below the fold, a clever heading nobody understands, no brand to remember. Most pages fail the same few ways, so most fixes are the same few moves.

The homepage has one job: a first-time visitor knows what this is, sees it working, and takes the next step. Everything on the page either helps that or gets in its way.

## How to use these rules

- Building or editing a homepage: hold every section to the rules below before calling it done.
- Critiquing one: use `review-homepage`, which runs these rules and writes the feedback in the format below.
- Look at the page at **1440×900** (desktop) and **390×844** (phone). "Above the fold" means inside those frames, with no scroll.
- When the site is church, ministry or Bible software, load `faith-homepage` too.
- For reference sites to point people at, see [REFERENCES.md](REFERENCES.md).

## The rules

### 1. Pass the five-second test

Cover everything below the fold. Can a stranger say what this is, who it's for and what to do next? If the honest answer is "what is this, and what am I supposed to do?", nothing further down the page matters yet.

- **Check:** the heading, one supporting line, the primary button and a product visual all sit inside the first frame on desktop and phone.
- **Fix:** say the plain thing first. Save the poetry for the second line or the second section.

### 2. Say it plainly

A clever heading makes the visitor do work. Write the obvious version, then make it sharp — Harry Dry and Jason Fried are the people to study here.

- No insider words without a definition. If your audience might not know a term, either use the plain version or explain it in a short line.
- The supporting text stays at **three lines or fewer on desktop**.
- Drop the eyebrow (the small label above the heading) unless it says something the heading doesn't. Often the eyebrow's words belong in the heading.
- If your best line is buried in section four, it's probably your heading.

### 3. Show the product, high up

Put the real product just below the primary button, in the first frame. A screenshot, a stylized slice of the UI or a device mockup does more than any sentence about it.

- **Not** a video that has to be pressed to show anything.
- **Not** a background photo that could belong to any company.
- **Not** a mockup so busy that the visitor has to study it to work out what it is.
- For a simple app, the app icon in the center with a short heading, one straightforward paragraph and a download button can be enough.
- If the product is a reader, a search or a directory, let people see it or use it in the hero. That's the shortest route to "I get it".

### 4. One primary action, and the shortest path to value

One button carries the page. Everything else is quieter.

- Don't ask for a name, an account or five onboarding steps before people have seen anything worth giving them to.
- Email products: take the address and send the first useful email straight away. Any extra onboarding goes in that email, and it's optional.
- Two app stores: one button that offers both beats two equal buttons side by side. If the app is iOS only, say so and show the Apple mark.

### 5. Every section shows something

Text-only sections are where attention dies. FAQ and pricing fine print are the exceptions. Everything else needs a visual that carries the point.

- **Check:** cover the text in each section. Is there still something that says what the section is about?
- **Fix:** use `show-dont-tell`. Stylized UI from the app, a real screenshot, an icon grid, photography, mixed media, or a mockup of the outcome, such as a calendar, an inbox or a finished document.

### 6. Build from your strongest section

Almost every page already has one section that works: clear, visual, specific, unmistakably this product. That section is the brief for the rest of the page.

- If it's stronger than the hero, it becomes the hero.
- If it isn't hero material, rebuild the weaker sections in its image: same visual language, same density, same clarity.
- Use `find-strongest-section` to name it and plan the move.

### 7. Vary the rhythm, structure the visuals

Ten sections that all look the same (background image, UI card, paragraph) read as one long section. Vary the shape: a grid of icons with short text, a large single screenshot, a quote, a comparison, a slider.

- Screenshots sit on a grid with intent. Thrown at a colored background at random angles, they look unfinished.
- Alignment and gaps follow a system. Misalignment that's off by a few pixels reads as carelessness, not style.

### 8. Features as a scannable grid

Show the core features as icon + short heading + one line. Use the same icons the app uses, so the homepage previews the product. A slider works when each item needs a visual.

- Three to six features on the homepage. The full platform goes on its own page, with a link.
- Turn a wall of checkmarks into a breakdown with real screenshots, one feature per row.

### 9. Less homepage

Most homepages have too many sections, in the wrong order. Order by what a first-time visitor needs: what it is → see it → how it works → why this one → proof → start.

- Cut sections that repeat a section above them.
- Side information in a second column fights the main column. Make it a small link that opens a dialog.
- A simple idea deserves a simple page. A directory needs search and filters in the hero and very little else.
- Use `cut-homepage` to make the cut list.

### 10. Motion never hides the content

Motion on a marketing page earns its place by explaining something. It loses it when people have to wait or chase.

- A marquee of items moving past is hard to read. A grid or list that sits still beats it.
- Scroll-jacked and scroll-triggered sections that hold content back cost more than they show. Keep them small, or drop them.
- A decorative hero animation that pulls focus from the heading and supporting text is a distraction.

### 11. One brand, carried through

A page with great bones but no personality gets described by what it connects to, not by its name. Pick the brand element that already works — the app icon, a color, a handmade type treatment, a motif in the logo — and carry it through the page in small ways.

- The wordmark is sized with the icon: noticeably smaller than the icon's height, not matching it, not a footnote beside it.
- Avoid display fonts so overused they read as a template, and serifs too thin to hold at heading size. Fontshare is a good free library.
- If the palette has a color, use it with confidence. A bare page with one purple button isn't a brand yet.
- A dark theme is a choice. If the audience expects daylight, explore a light version inspired by the app icon.
- Use `brand-pass` to work through it.

### 12. Real over generated

AI-generated imagery with garbled text, wrong details or a generic sheen costs trust, and now more than ever visitors notice. Real product shots, real people and real places carry more weight than polished images that aren't true.

- No AI images of people, scripture, documents or UI that contain readable text.
- Visual interest is part of trust. A page with none looks like it took an afternoon.

### 13. Position yourself as the best at your thing

Say what you are in the category people already search for, then why you're the better one.

- If you're built on or for another platform, lead with what you do, not the integration. Otherwise people will call you "the app for X" and never learn your name.
- Steer away from a category you don't want to be judged in. A prayer app that sounds like a social network gets compared to social networks.
- A "Why us" section that names what's different beats a list of what's included.

### 14. Your why, after the what

A short founder note — who made this and why — turns a product into something people root for. Keep it after the visitor understands what the product is and how it works, and keep it about them as much as you.

### 15. Ready to be shared

The link will be pasted in chats and posts before most people visit.

- The Open Graph image is often the best expression of the brand. Make the page live up to it, or make it live up to the page.
- Title, meta description, favicon and Open Graph image are all set and specific.

## Giving feedback with these rules

Feedback on a homepage follows the same shape every time. `review-homepage` uses it.

1. **Start with what's working.** Name it specifically. People can only keep doing what they know works.
2. **Name the strongest section** and what to do with it.
3. **Up to seven changes**, most important first, each one specific enough to act on. "I'd explore…" for directions, plain imperatives for fixes.
4. **Two or three reference sites**, distinct from each other, each with what to take from it.
5. **No pile-on.** If there are twenty problems, pick the seven that unlock the rest.

## Review checklist

| Issue | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Can't tell what it is in five seconds | Plain heading, one supporting line, button and product visual in the first frame |
| Clever or insider heading | The obvious version first; define the jargon |
| Supporting text over three lines on desktop | Cut to three or fewer |
| Eyebrow repeating the heading | Remove it, or fold its words into the heading |
| Hero video or background photo instead of the product | Screenshots or device mockups just below the button |
| Mockup you have to study | One clear view, or the app icon with a straight paragraph |
| Name or sign-up before value | Let people start first; ask later |
| Two equal store buttons | One button with both options, or one platform named plainly |
| Text-only sections | A visual per section (`show-dont-tell`) |
| Weak hero, strong section further down | Promote it, or rebuild the rest in its image (`find-strongest-section`) |
| Every section the same shape | Vary it: grid, single shot, quote, comparison, slider |
| Screenshots placed at random | Put them on a grid with intent |
| A long feature list | Three to six as icon + heading + line; the full list on its own page |
| Too many sections, or out of order | What → see → how → why → proof → start (`cut-homepage`) |
| Side column fighting the main one | A link that opens a dialog |
| Marquee, scroll-jacking or distracting hero motion | A still grid; shorten or remove the motion |
| No personality, visuals feel random | Carry one brand element through the page (`brand-pass`) |
| Wordmark matching or dwarfed by the icon | Size the wordmark well under the icon height |
| Overused or thin display font | Choose a typeface with character that holds at size |
| AI imagery with wrong text | Real product shots, people and places |
| Sold as "the app for another platform" | Lead with your own category and name |
| No founder or why | A short note, after the what and how |
| Bland or missing share card | An Open Graph image that carries the brand |

## Tone

Warm and direct. Open with something true and good about the page. Then be specific: name the section, the line, the size. Say "I'd explore" when there's more than one right answer, and say it flat when there's one. Never pad the list to look thorough.
